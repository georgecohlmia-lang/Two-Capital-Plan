STEP ONE · PLAN BASICS

A few details up front.

The plan you build here serves two audiences with the same document: the people whose lives are tied to your decisions today, and the investor who'll read this version of you a year or two from now.

THE PRINCIPLE THAT GOVERNS THE WHOLE PLAN
A capital plan that lives only in your head isn't a plan. It's an assumption.
Reassurances aren't plans. "Trust me, I've got this" isn't information — it's a feeling dressed up as one. When you walk into a room with a written document, the conversation shifts from "are you okay?" to "let me understand this." Spouses become thinking partners. Advisors become participants. Investors see a owner who's done the work. The plan doesn't have to be sophisticated. It has to be honest.

A single planning horizon makes the math clearer. 12 months is the standard for early-stage; some owners prefer 24.

STEP THREE · RISKS & CONTINGENCIES

Three or four specific risks. With responses already written.

This is the section most owners leave out — and the one that builds the most trust. Stay away from "market conditions" — that means nothing. Name specific things that could undermine the plan, and what you'd do if they materialize.

WHAT QUALIFIES AS A SPECIFIC RISK · WHAT DOESN'T
QUALIFIES: "The founding client cohort takes twice as long to fill." "The sales team hits break-even later than modeled." "The manufacturer changes payment terms."

DOES NOT: "Market conditions." "Macro environment." "Competition." Vague risks produce vague responses — and lose the reader's trust.
Risk 01

Three sentences is enough. What's the immediate response? What's the timeline? What triggers the next decision?

"We extend the timeline by 90 days and draw on the established credit facility to bridge payroll. If the cohort isn't filled within those 90 days, we revisit team cost structure before adding any new spend."
